Why Chanel Doesn't Advertise Like Everyone Else

Open Instagram right now and count how many ads you scroll past in five minutes. Chanel keeps its footprint there deliberately small. The house skips performance ads and avoids chasing search rankings or trend-driven content, letting brand equity carry the weight instead of paid reach (Digital Agency Network).

Chanel doesn't compete for clicks because it doesn't need to. The brand pulled in $19.7 billion in global revenue in 2023. That's a 16% increase from the year before, and Chanel built that growth on storytelling and brand support activities instead of a typical ad campaign (Chanel 2023 Financial Results).

How Chanel Used to Sell Itself

The first Chanel No. 5 ad ran in Harper's Bazaar in 1937. It featured Gabrielle "Coco" Chanel herself, photographed to promote the fragrance she created (BANG!). By 1971, the ads moved to color photography. They captured a windswept, free-spirited mood that matched the decade. But the campaign most people picture when they think of Chanel came from Richard Avedon in the 1970s. It starred French actress Catherine Deneuve, with just her face, the bottle, her name, and the Chanel name (Fashionista). That minimalism became the template. Carole Bouquet took over in the 1980s with equally simple print ads and commercials directed by Ridley Scott (StyleCaster).

What Replaced the Ad Campaign

The turning point came in 2004. Chanel spent a reported $33 million on a short film that ran three minutes. Nicole Kidman starred, Baz Luhrmann directed, and Chanel called it "No. 5, The Film" (Creativepool). It was a cinematic event, and it set the tone for everything Chanel does now, whether that be runway spectacle, ambassador storytelling, or experiences built to generate press coverage. The rest of this site breaks down how that strategy plays out today, and what it means for the future of luxury marketing.
